Self-check

Introduction to generalized linear mixed models

Nine short questions on the sample session, two or three per learning goal. You get two tries per question, then the answer and an explanation. Anonymous: nothing is stored or sent anywhere.

Goal 1: Recognising grouped data

1. A researcher records 40 trials from each of 12 macaques and compares two conditions with a t-test on all 480 trials. What is the main problem?

The 480 trials are not 480 independent pieces of information: trials from the same animal are more alike than trials from different animals. Treating them as independent overstates the evidence (pseudoreplication). A mixed model with a random effect for macaque accounts for this.

2. Which of these datasets is not hierarchical?

If every lizard has a different mother, there are no clusters: each observation comes from its own “group”. All the other examples have repeated observations within a higher-level unit (infant, social group, session, monkey).

Goal 2: Random intercepts and the ICC

3. In lmer(dors ~ sex + (1 | mother), data = lizard), what does (1 | mother) do?

(1 | mother) is a random intercept: each mother gets her own baseline \(\alpha_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{mother})\). The model estimates how much mothers vary (\(\sigma^2_{mother}\)) instead of a separate parameter for each one. Letting the effect of sex differ between mothers would be a random slope, (sex | mother).

4. The variance between mothers is 1.78 and the residual variance is 2.25. What is the intraclass correlation (ICC)?

\(\rho = \dfrac{\sigma^2_{mother}}{\sigma^2_{mother} + \sigma^2} = \dfrac{1.78}{1.78 + 2.25} = 0.44\). About 44% of the variability in dorsal scales is due to differences between mothers.

5. What does an ICC close to 0 tell you?

An ICC near 0 means the clusters explain almost none of the variability, so the grouping matters little. An ICC near 1 would mean observations within a cluster are nearly identical. The ICC says nothing about the fixed effects.

Goal 3: Choosing between an LMM and a GLMM

6. On each of 20 trials, you record whether an infant looked at the target (1) or not (0). Which model fits best?

The response is binary, so we need a binomial family with a logit link (a GLM), and the trials are grouped within infants, so we need a random effect (a mixed model). Together: a GLMM. The glm() ignores the grouping; lmer() and lm() assume a continuous, normally distributed response.

7. You count the number of aggressive interactions of each individual per week, over 10 weeks. Which model is the natural starting point?

Counts call for a Poisson family with a log link, or a negative binomial if the counts are overdispersed. Repeated weeks from the same individual call for a random intercept for individual.

Goal 4: Interpreting a GLMM

8. In a logistic GLMM, the estimate for session is 0.50. What does this mean?

Coefficients in a logistic model are on the log-odds scale: \(e^{0.50} \approx 1.65\) is the odds ratio per session. In a GLMM, this is the effect for an individual with random effect \(b_i = 0\), the typical individual, not the population average.

9. Why is the population-average curve flatter than the curve of the typical individual in a logistic GLMM?

Each individual has its own S-shaped curve, shifted by its random intercept. Averaging these curves at each value of the predictor gives a curve that rises more gently than any single one. In a linear mixed model the two coincide; with a non-linear link such as the logit, they do not.
